# AI Ethics

Powerful technology comes with responsibility attached, and in health that
responsibility is not abstract. An AI system's decision affects a real person's
care. This session is where you learn to build responsibly, not as a
compliance exercise at the end, but as a discipline that should already have
shaped every project you have built so far in this curriculum.

```{note}
**Level** All levels. **Prerequisite** [Clinical AI](../health/clinical-ai.md)
is helpful context.
**Time** ~2 hours. **Sessions** 1.
**Before you start** Your capstone project idea, even in rough form.
```

## What you will be able to do

1. Name the four core ethical issues in clinical AI and give a concrete example
   of each.
2. Place a project on a three-level risk framework and name the safeguard each
   level requires.
3. Run the Responsible AI Checklist against your own capstone project honestly.
4. Explain who is accountable when an AI-assisted decision goes wrong.

## The four core issues

| Issue | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Bias and fairness** | Bias in data and models can amplify existing health inequities. Check performance across patient groups, not only on average. |
| **Privacy and consent** | Patient data requires consent and protection under Thailand's PDPA and international standards, covered fully in [Digital Health](../digital-health.md). |
| **Copyright and data rights** | Respect the rights attached to the data you train on, including data generated by other AI systems, which carries its own provenance questions. |
| **Accountability** | Who is responsible when the AI is wrong? This must be answered clearly before real use, never improvised afterward. |

```{important}
These four are not separate boxes to tick once. Bias shows up again every time
your data shifts. Consent needs revisiting if a project's scope changes. Treat
this as an ongoing practice, the same discipline you carried through
[Clinical AI](../health/clinical-ai.md) and [Governance](../governance.md).
```

## A risk framework you can actually use

| Risk level | Looks like | Required safeguard |
|---|---|---|
| **Low** | A support tool that does not decide anything for a person | Normal review process |
| **Medium** | Assists a clinical decision | Human-in-the-loop is mandatory, not optional |
| **High** | Directly affects diagnosis or treatment | Clinical trial and formal governance, matching the SaMD path in [Governance](../governance.md) |

Placing your own project honestly on this table, before you build further, is
one of the most useful five minutes you will spend on it. It tells you exactly
what safeguard you are not allowed to skip.

## The Responsible AI Checklist

```{important}
Run this against your own capstone project, honestly, before you consider it
finished:
- [ ] I checked for bias in the data and in the results, across patient groups.
- [ ] Consent was obtained and de-identification was done correctly.
- [ ] The model is explainable to a level a clinician can actually use.
- [ ] There is a monitoring plan for after deployment.
- [ ] It is clear who is accountable, and what happens, if something goes wrong.
```

If any line is unchecked, that is not a footnote. It is the next thing to fix
before the project is ready to show anyone outside your own testing.

```{tip}
This checklist connects directly to the strategic and regulatory view in
[Strategy and Governance](../governance.md), and to the
[Hospital information pathway](../../pathways/hospital.md) if your interest
runs toward policy as much as building.
```

## Common mistakes

- **Treating ethics as a week seven topic** instead of a constraint present
  from the first design decision, all the way back to
  [Basics](../basics.md).
- **Checking bias once, at the start,** instead of every time the data or the
  deployment context changes.
- **Leaving accountability vague.** "The team" is not an answer. A specific
  role or person should be named before real use.
- **Assuming a checklist completed once stays valid forever.** Revisit it
  whenever scope, data, or users change.

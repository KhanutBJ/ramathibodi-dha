# Deciding where AI belongs: a strategist's view

Everything so far in Governance has been about the regulatory path: can this
tool be approved. This lesson is a different question, asked earlier: should
an institution build it, buy it, or wait, and how does a leader make that call
well. This is the competency a department head or hospital executive needs,
distinct from the regulatory literacy every builder needs too.

```{note}
**Level** Intermediate. **Prerequisite** [Strategy and Governance](../governance.md).
**Time** ~2.5 hours. **Sessions** 1.
**Before you start** A real or hypothetical AI investment decision facing
your own department or institution.
```

## What you will be able to do

1. Frame a build-versus-buy decision for a clinical AI tool with the right questions.
2. Estimate the real cost of "buy," including the dependency it creates.
3. Evaluate a vendor pitch without being sold to.
4. Plan the change management a new tool needs to actually get used.

## Build, buy, or wait

Three options, and "wait" is a real, often correct answer:

- **Build.** You get exactly what you need and the capability stays in the
  institution, at the cost of time, talent, and risk you own.
- **Buy.** Faster to deploy, but you inherit the vendor's roadmap, pricing,
  and support quality, and the capability leaves when the contract ends.
- **Wait.** Sometimes the right call. A tool bought or built before the
  workflow, the data, or the team is ready usually fails quietly, wasting the
  investment and the goodwill needed for the next attempt.

```{tip}
Ask the question in [Why Thailand should train builders, not just buyers](../../insights/train-builders-not-buyers.html)
at the institutional scale: what happens to this capability in three years, if
we buy versus if we build.
```

## Evaluating a vendor without being sold to

A vendor pitch is optimised to close, not to inform. A strategist asks the
questions the deck does not answer:

- **What is the intended use, precisely**, in the language of
  [Governance](../governance.md). If the answer is vague, the regulatory
  status likely is too.
- **What evidence backs the claim**, and was it validated on a population
  anything like yours.
- **What happens when it is wrong.** Ask for the failure mode, not just the
  headline accuracy.
- **What do you own when the contract ends.** Data, models, and institutional
  knowledge should not all walk out the door with the vendor.

```{warning}
A polished demo is not evidence. Ask what happens on the cases the demo did
not show you, and watch how directly the vendor answers.
```

## The real cost of "buy" is not the invoice

Licensing fees are the visible cost. The invisible ones compound: staff time
adapting workflows to fit the tool, dependency on the vendor's update cycle,
and the erosion of your own team's ability to build the next thing, because
the institution never practised. Weigh all three before comparing sticker
prices.

## Change management, or the tool will not be used

The best-evaluated tool fails if the people meant to use it were not part of
deciding to adopt it. A strategist plans for this as deliberately as for the
budget:

1. **Involve the users who will actually touch it**, before the decision, not
   after.
2. **Pilot narrowly**, in one ward or one clinic, before an institution-wide
   rollout.
3. **Measure adoption, not just accuracy.** A tool nobody opens has zero
   clinical value regardless of its AUROC.
4. **Name an owner** who is accountable for the tool's use after the launch
   excitement fades.

```{important}
This is where the "clinic first" principle from [Who We Are](../../who-we-are.html)
becomes an executive discipline, not just a builder's habit: the workflow and
the people using it come before the technology, at every scale.
```

## Common mistakes

- **Comparing tools on accuracy alone**, ignoring integration cost, vendor
  dependency, and change management.
- **Skipping the pilot** and rolling out institution-wide on day one.
- **No named owner** after deployment, so the tool degrades unnoticed.
- **Treating "wait" as failure to decide**, when it is sometimes the correct,
  disciplined choice.

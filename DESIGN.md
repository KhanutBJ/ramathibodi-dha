# Design language

The name of our design language is **Clinical Light**.

It comes from one idea: the work is serious, so the surface should be calm. A
hospital is loud, fluorescent, and crowded. Our screens are the opposite. They
give the eye room to think. The restraint is not a style choice. It is respect
for people who do hard work and do not need a website shouting at them.

Four lineages meet here, and we owe each of them.

- **IBM Carbon** gives us discipline: a grid, a type scale, components that
  behave. Nothing is arbitrary.
- **Dieter Rams** gives us subtraction: as little design as possible, every
  element earning its place. "Good design is as little design as possible."
- **Apple under Jony Ive** gives us clarity and material honesty: deep type,
  real hierarchy, motion that explains rather than decorates.
- **The editorial journal** (Sequoia, YC, the Steve Jobs Archive) gives us
  voice: a page can read like a well-set piece of writing, not a product
  screen. Type with character, one earned mark per page, a hand that
  occasionally shows.

What makes it ours, not a copy of those, is the soul underneath.

## The soul

**A single line, drawn once.** The logo is a brain drawn as one continuous,
looping line, the way a confident hand sketches an idea without lifting the pen.
That is the feeling we build everywhere: one clear gesture, drawn with intent.
The gradient that runs along it, orange to indigo, is the only place we let
colour sing. Everywhere else, ink and paper.

**Warm rigor.** Carbon and Rams can feel cold. We are a club born in a Thai
hospital, made of people who care for patients. So the geometry is precise but
the voice is human, the corners are softened, the light is warm. Rigor you can
sit next to.

**Earned colour.** The brand gradient is a reward, not wallpaper. It appears once
per view at most: a single word in a headline, one primary button, the brain
mark. When everything could glow, nothing means anything. Scarcity is what makes
the colour feel like a heartbeat.

**The bedside, not the lab.** Imagery and language stay close to care. We say
"to the bedside," not "end to end." The work is measured in patients helped.

**Show, don't announce.** We do not put our positioning in a sentence and set it
in a handwritten font. If the work feels like a new way of thinking, that comes
from the diagrams, the restraint, and the craft, not from a label that says so.

## The two voices

The site speaks in two registers, on purpose, and keeps them separate.

- **The typeset voice** (Fraunces headlines, Newsreader reading text, Montserrat
  labels) is the argument: confident, edited, considered. This is what carries
  claims, numbers, and anything a reader needs to trust.
- **The handwritten voice** (Caveat) is the margin note: a short phrase above a
  headline, a signature in the footer, a caption on a diagram. It never carries
  a claim, a number, or the site's positioning. It is texture, used sparingly,
  never the message itself.

## Our art: the hand-sketch diagram

Where a page would otherwise need a paragraph, we draw instead. A diagram in
this system is: shapes wobbled by a light SVG turbulence filter (so lines read
as drawn, not vector-perfect), one gradient spent at the single most important
point, mono labels for structure, and an optional handwritten caption for the
narrative beat. The hospital-workflow diagram on What We Do and the nested-rings
motif on Who We Are are the reference examples. Every major page should earn one
of these before it earns another paragraph.

## Real marks over text

Where an organisation has a real, findable logo, we use it, not a text pill.
Marks render grayscale by default and gain colour on hover (the same restraint
as earned colour above): present, but not shouting for attention next to our
own brand. Where no official asset exists, we use a clean typographic chip in
our own system rather than fabricate or approximate someone else's mark.

## Motion, invented, not borrowed

Two moments are ours specifically, not a template's default:

- **The ink rail.** A hand-drawn reading-progress line, fixed to the left edge,
  that fills as you move through a page and ends in a small drawn dot. A
  reader's finger tracing the page, made physical.
- **The drawn underline.** Navigation and inline links do not get a filled pill
  on hover. A thin line draws itself in underneath, the way you would underline
  something by hand to mark it as important.

Everything else (reveals, the diagram stroke-draw, the accent-rule grow-in) is
quiet and single-purpose: it confirms you arrived, then gets out of the way.

## The rules

1. **Two surfaces, one system.** The public site and the Academy reader share the
   same tokens, type, and spacing. Learning should feel like the same place as
   the front door.
2. **Light and dark are equals.** Neither is an afterthought. The default follows
   the reader's system, and the toggle is always one tap away.
3. **Type is the design.** Fraunces for headlines, Newsreader for reading,
   Montserrat for UI labels, IBM Plex Mono for structure and code, Caveat for
   the margin voice only. Big, tight headlines. Generous, readable prose.
4. **Less text, more diagram.** If a section can be a drawing with two labels
   instead of a paragraph, it should be. Cut before you add.
5. **Whitespace is content.** Sections breathe. We would rather cut a paragraph
   than crowd one.
6. **Motion with a reason.** Things arrive once, quietly, and our two invented
   moments (the ink rail, the drawn underline) are the only motion that calls
   attention to itself. No carousels, no parallax theatre.
7. **Honesty over polish.** We show the denominator, the confidence interval, the
   population a model was tested on. Trust is a design material.
8. **No slop.** No em dashes, no middle dots, no filler. Human prose, or nothing.
   And no telling the reader what to think of us. Show it.

## Why it has soul

Soul is not an effect you add at the end. It is the result of a point of view
held consistently. Ours: medicine is serious and people are tired, so be calm,
be clear, be warm, draw more than we write, and let one line and one gradient
carry the feeling. Do that everywhere, and the site stops looking designed and
starts feeling inevitable.
